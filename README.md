# Wake-on-Energy: Energy-as-a-Sensor IoT with On-Demand Wake-Up
DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.17266888

## Introduction

Swansea is building a Net Zero future, targeting clean air, energy-efficient buildings and sustainable infrastructure. Achieving these ambitions requires distributed environmental intelligence without the energy consumption, installation costs and maintenance burden associated with conventional sensor networks.

**Wake-on-Energy is a proposed battery-free IoT architecture in which harvested energy serves two purposes: powering distributed sensor nodes and providing an implicit source of information about their surrounding environment.**

The central idea is that energy harvesting can reveal more than the availability of electrical power. Energy generated by physical processes associated with human activity can also provide information about those processes. Examples include waste heat from televisions, computers and household appliances, light from illuminated spaces, and mechanical energy from movement. The availability, accumulation and temporal patterns of harvested energy can therefore provide an additional indication of activity in the surrounding environment, even before conventional sensor measurements are requested.

The network combines this **energy-as-information principle** with selective, hub-controlled sensing. Nodes operate in ultra-low-power states and communicate only when explicitly interrogated by a central hub through a dedicated Radio Controlii wake-up receiver. Their ability to respond depends on their available stored energy and operational state.

Rather than indiscriminately polling every node, the hub uses Bayesian inference and information-driven selection to prioritise nodes expected to provide the greatest additional information. Node energy history, availability and response behaviour contribute to this decision. A non-responsive node is not automatically classified as inactive: its silence is treated as an uncertain observation that must be interpreted in the context of its previous energy and communication history.

**The central contribution of Wake-on-Energy is the integration of energy harvesting as both a power source and an implicit information channel, with intelligent, selective interrogation of distributed sensor nodes.** The energy sustaining the network also helps determine where and when the network should obtain its next measurement.

The longer-term ambition is to support distributed environmental monitoring and building-energy management across Swansea's Net Zero infrastructure, including building utilisation, thermal efficiency, environmental conditions and adaptive heating.

## How It Works

### 1. Energy harvesting and local availability

Distributed nodes harvest energy from locally available sources, potentially including:

- **Thermoelectric generation (TEG):** harvesting energy from temperature differences, including waste heat from appliances, equipment and heating systems.
- **Organic photovoltaic (OPV):** harvesting energy from indoor or outdoor light.
- **Vibration harvesting:** converting mechanical movement and vibration into electrical energy.

Harvested energy is accumulated in a supercapacitor buffer. The node's energy state, charging behaviour and ability to become operational provide information about the local energy environment.

The energy-harvesting system therefore has two roles:

1. Supplying the energy required for node operation.
2. Providing an additional, low-resolution information channel about local physical activity and environmental conditions.

Energy availability is not assumed to be a direct measurement of human occupancy. It is interpreted alongside other observations and known environmental influences.

### 2. Ultra-low-power operation and on-demand wake-up

Nodes remain in an ultra-low-power state when not required. A dedicated Radio Controlii wake-up receiver allows the hub to request a node's attention without requiring the main microcontroller and sensor subsystem to remain continuously active.

In this architecture, the wake-up receiver shares the primary supercapacitor power rail with the main ATmega4808 microcontroller and XBee radio. **This design enforces true zero standby drain:** when harvested energy is exhausted, the wake-up receiver powers down completely alongside the rest of the node. 

Furthermore, energy harvesters (such as Epishine OPV modules) naturally require supercapacitor voltage to cross specific operational thresholds before delivering sustained power. This prevents low-voltage brownout loops, ensuring that nodes remain entirely dormant until sufficient energy is banked to guarantee atomic execution of wake-up, sampling, and transmission tasks.

When a valid wake-up request is received, and sufficient energy is available, the node activates its subsystem according to this operating sequence:

1. Harvest and store energy past the harvester's operational threshold.
2. Maintain the node (including the wake-up receiver) unpowered or in ultra-low-power sleep.
3. Receive a wake-up request when energy permits the receiver to operate.
4. Activate the microcontroller and sensor subsystem.
5. Acquire and transmit the requested measurements.
6. Return to the low-power or unpowered state.

Nodes do not routinely transmit data or participate in continuous polling, significantly reducing unnecessary communication overhead.

### 3. Energy as an implicit sensing channel

A node's energy state and response behaviour provide information independently of its conventional sensor measurements.

For example:

- **Successful response:** indicates that the node has sufficient energy and is operational. Its sensor measurements can then provide more detailed environmental information.
- **Failure to respond:** indicates that the node was unavailable or did not complete the requested communication. Because the wake-up receiver shares the same energy rail, total silence can signify that the node is completely starved of energy due to sustained inactivity in its micro-zone (e.g., dark, cold, or static environment).

The hub must distinguish between low energy availability, communication failure and other possible causes of non-response. Previous charging behaviour, stored-energy estimates, response history and network reliability help interpret these observations.

This is an important distinction: **silence is information, but it is not automatically proof of inactivity.**

### 4. Bayesian information-driven node selection

The central hub maintains information about the network and uses it to determine which node or nodes should be interrogated next.

The intended selection process considers:

- Previous measurements and node response history.
- Energy availability and charging behaviour.
- Uncertainty about local environmental conditions.
- The expected information gain from obtaining another measurement.
- The energy and communication cost associated with interrogation.

Rather than polling every node at fixed intervals, the hub prioritises nodes expected to provide the greatest additional information relative to the resources required to obtain it.

This is an adaptive sensing strategy: the network uses what it already knows to decide where further measurements are most valuable.

The precise optimisation method and its performance will be established through implementation and experimental evaluation. Bayesian active sensing and value-of-information methods provide a suitable framework for this approach.

### 5. High-resolution environmental measurements

When a node is successfully activated, it can provide measurements from a BME680 or other compatible low-power sensors.

The BME680 provides:

- Temperature.
- Relative humidity.
- Atmospheric pressure.
- Gas resistance, which can be used as an indicator of changes in volatile organic compound (VOC) levels.

The BME680 does not directly identify individual gases or pollutants. Its gas-resistance output requires appropriate interpretation and should not be treated as a direct measurement of pollutant concentration.

These higher-resolution measurements complement the information available from harvested energy and node response behaviour.

### 6. Data collection and visualisation

The hub collects and records available sensor measurements, node status and energy-related observations.

Over time, these data may be used to investigate:

- Building utilisation and patterns of activity.
- Local temperature and humidity variations.
- Thermal behaviour and potential heat inefficiencies.
- Changes in gas-resistance indicators.
- The relationship between energy availability and environmental conditions.
- The effectiveness of selective interrogation compared with conventional polling.

The combination of energy-derived information and selectively acquired sensor measurements provides a multi-level view of the monitored environment.

## System Architecture

The proposed architecture consists of three principal elements.

### 1. Sensor Nodes (Slaves)

Distributed, energy-harvesting nodes positioned around buildings or other monitored environments.

**Hardware:**

- ATmega4808 microcontroller.
- BME680 environmental sensor.
- Matrix Prometheus thermoelectric generator (TEG).
- Epishine organic photovoltaic (OPV) cell.
- Xidas VP3-20/30/50/60 vibration energy-harvesting modules.
- Supercapacitor energy-storage buffer (shared power rail supplying MCU, XBee, and Radio Controlii).
- Radio Controlii wake-up receiver (868 MHz variant).
- Digi XBee S2C End Device for data communication.

**Responsibilities:**

- Harvest and store locally available energy.
- Monitor or maintain an estimate of energy availability.
- Remain in an ultra-low-power or unpowered state when not required.
- Respond to hub-issued wake-up requests when operational.
- Acquire and transmit environmental measurements.
- Return to a low-power state after completing the requested operation.

### 2. Routers (Overseers)

Strategically positioned network nodes that provide communication coverage and forward data between sensor nodes and the central hub.

**Hardware:**

- ATmega4808 microcontroller.
- Digi XBee S2C Router.

**Responsibilities:**

- Maintain the XBee mesh network.
- Forward messages between sensor nodes and the hub.
- Support communication across a distributed deployment.

Routers are part of the communications infrastructure; they are not assumed to provide environmental measurements unless separately equipped to do so.

### 3. Central Hub (Master)

The central controller coordinates node interrogation, data collection and network-level decision-making.

**Hardware and software:**

- Digi XBee S2C Coordinator.
- USB connection to a PC or Raspberry Pi.
- Python-based hub software.

**Responsibilities:**

- Issue wake-up requests to selected nodes.
- Receive and record node responses.
- Maintain node availability and measurement histories.
- Evaluate uncertainty and expected information gain.
- Prioritise subsequent node interrogations.
- Log and visualise environmental measurements.
- Evaluate the effectiveness of the selective sensing strategy.

The intended control strategy is Bayesian and information-driven. Machine learning may be incorporated where supported by the data and a clearly defined predictive task; it is not assumed to be necessary for every part of the architecture.
